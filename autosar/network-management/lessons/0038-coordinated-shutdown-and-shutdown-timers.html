Network Management

Coordinated shutdown and the shutdown timers

When nobody needs any bus of the cluster any more, the NM coordinator does not release all of them at once. It holds each bus a little longer, so that the slow buses and the fast buses all reach Bus-Sleep Mode at the same moment.

Lesson 37 showed how the coordinator keeps the buses of an NM coordination cluster awake. This lesson shows the other half: how it lets them go, and how the delays per bus are calculated.

When the shutdown starts

The coordinator checks all the time whether every bus of the cluster is either ready to sleep or already in Bus-Sleep Mode. "Ready to sleep" means two things for a bus: ComM has released it with Nm_NetworkRelease, and its bus NM has reported a Remote Sleep Indication (no other ECU on that bus still requests it). As soon as this is true for the whole cluster, the coordinator starts the coordinated shutdown (releasing the awake buses of the cluster one by one, with a calculated delay each, so that they reach Bus-Sleep Mode at the same time) on all buses that are still awake.

Buses that are already asleep take no part; they are only watched. If the cluster has a synchronizing network, the coordinator waits for a suitable moment on it first; lesson 39 explains that case. The example gateway has none, so its shutdown starts at once.

Why the buses need different delays

A released bus does not fall asleep at once. With CanNm or UdpNm it first stays in Ready Sleep State until the NM-Timeout Time has run out, then in Prepare Bus-Sleep Mode for the Wait Bus-Sleep Time. The specification calls the sum, the time from the release to Bus-Sleep Mode, the shutdown time of the channel, TSHUTDOWN_CHANNEL (the time a bus needs from its release until it is in bus sleep; for CanNm and UdpNm the time in Ready Sleep State plus the time in Prepare Bus-Sleep Mode).

The three buses of the gateway have different timers, so their shutdown times differ. If the coordinator released all three at the same moment, the fastest bus would sleep first and the slowest last. To make them sleep together, it starts a shutdown timer (one timer per bus: the delay before the coordinator releases that bus) for every awake bus and releases the bus only when its timer expires.

The calculation

All timers are derived from one value for the whole coordination, the global coordinator time (the total time the coordinated shutdown may take, from its start until all buses sleep; it is configured in NmIf, see lesson 55). For each bus:

shutdown timer of a bus = global coordinator time − TSHUTDOWN_CHANNEL of that bus

The bus with the longest shutdown time gets the shortest timer, the fastest bus the longest one. Released this way, every bus reaches Bus-Sleep Mode when the global coordinator time is over. That only works if the global coordinator time is at least the longest shutdown time of all coordinated buses; the specification requires that. A global coordinator time of 0 makes every timer 0: all buses are released at once and fall asleep one after the other.

When a timer expires, NmIf first asks that bus NM for a bus synchronization and then releases the network on it. From then on the bus NM runs its normal way to sleep. NmIf keeps track of every bus it has released until the bus reports Bus-Sleep Mode; when all are released and all sleep, the coordinated shutdown is complete.

Exact, but not perfect

The specification says plainly that absolute synchronization cannot be reached. The coordinator and the bus NMs run in main function cycles, timers have a resolution, and on a bus that is not cyclic such as CAN or Ethernet, the moment of the last NM PDU before the release varies by up to one message cycle. Correctly configured, the buses fall asleep as close together as these limits allow. Making the global coordinator time longer than needed is allowed: all buses then simply stay awake longer before they sleep together.

Gateways below other gateways

A gateway can itself sit on a bus that another coordinator watches, for example a sub-gateway below the central gateway. Then the lower gateway must not start its shutdown on its own: it waits until the coordinator above tells it to. That signal is the NM Coordinator Sleep Ready Bit (a bit in the NM PDU with which a coordinator tells the coordinators below it to start their coordinated shutdown). Lesson 39 shows how it travels down the chain, and what happens when somebody needs a bus again in the middle of a shutdown.

Up to R24-11 (also in 4.4.0 and R20-11) a shutdown timer is started for every awake channel of the cluster. Since R25-11 the specification says: for every awake, actively coordinated channel. The difference only matters for a gateway with passively coordinated channels, which lesson 39 introduces. The formula is the same in all three releases.

Things to notice. The coordinator does not make the buses faster or slower; it only chooses when to release each one. The longest shutdown time decides the global coordinator time; every other bus waits the difference. The bus NMs do not know they are coordinated: each one gets a normal release, just later.
